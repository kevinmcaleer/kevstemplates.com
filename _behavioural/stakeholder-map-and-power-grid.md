Influence rarely matches the org chart. This finds who actually decides things.

---

## Step 1 — the long list

Include: the sponsor, budget holder, the leaders whose teams are affected, middle managers, informal opinion leaders, the union or works council if relevant, IT/Risk/Compliance/Legal gatekeepers, and anyone who lost an argument when this change was chosen.

⚠️ That last group is important and always missing from the list. Someone advocated a different solution and was overruled. They are not neutral, and they are usually senior.

## Step 3 — the grid

```
   HIGH │                    │
        │   KEEP SATISFIED   │   MANAGE CLOSELY
        │   (can kill it,    │   (your working
   I    │    not watching)   │    coalition)
   N    │                    │
   F  ──┼────────────────────┼────────────────────
   L    │                    │
   U    │   MONITOR          │   KEEP INFORMED
   E    │   (low effort)     │   (loud, low power —
   N    │                    │    but they set norms)
   C  LOW│                   │
        └────────────────────┴────────────────────
          LOW            INTEREST            HIGH
```

**The dangerous quadrant is top-left.** High influence, currently not paying attention. A Risk director or a regional MD who hasn't engaged yet, who will engage the moment something goes wrong, at maximum volume, with no context. Brief them *before* they need to care.

**The under-rated quadrant is bottom-right.** Low formal power, high interest, often highly networked. They set the informal norm. See [Social Norms and Influence](/behavioural-change/social-norms-and-influence/).

## Step 4 — the moves

| For a… | Do this |
|---|---|
| **Champion** | Give them a visible role and something concrete to do. Unused champions cool off. |
| **Supporter** | Ask for one specific public act, not general support. |
| **Neutral** | Find out what they want from the change. Neutrality is usually "nobody's told me what's in it for my area." |
| **Sceptic** | Meet privately, listen properly, and find the part where they're right. Concede it publicly. |
| **Blocker** | Understand what they're protecting. Most blocking is defensive, not hostile. Escalate only when you've genuinely tried. |
{:class="table table-single table-narrow"}

<div class="bct-callout bct-tip" markdown="1">
**The best move with a senior sceptic**

Ask them to define the conditions under which they'd support it. Write those conditions down and read them back. Either you get a workable list — in which case you've converted them and gained a design review — or they can't produce one, which tells the room something without you having to say it.
</div>


## Step 5 — the coalition

You don't need everyone. You need a **guiding coalition** with enough authority, credibility and expertise to make decisions stick.

## Step 6 — the influence question

For your top blocker: **whose opinion do *they* actually listen to?**

<div class="bct-callout bct-note" markdown="1">

The indirect route: `___________`

Working the indirect route is often faster than the direct one, and doesn't cost you the relationship.

---

## Refresh cadence

Stakeholder maps rot. Review monthly, and always after: a reorg, a departure, a bad incident, or budget season. A single leadership change can invalidate the whole grid.
