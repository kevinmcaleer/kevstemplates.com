William Bridges' distinction is the one insight most change plans are missing:

> **Change is situational. Transition is psychological. Change happens on a date. Transition takes as long as it takes.**

The new system goes live on the 14th. The people using it may still be in transition in March. Programme plans track the change; nobody tracks the transition, and then everyone is surprised when go-live is a mess.

---

## The three phases

### 1. Endings — letting go
What people lose: a familiar tool, a routine that made them look competent, a team, a sense of expertise, status, a relationship with a system they'd mastered.

**Signs:** nostalgia, "we tried this in 2019", anger, bargaining, quiet disengagement, sudden interest in other roles.

**What helps:**
- **Name the loss out loud.** The most under-used move in corporate change. "You've been the person everyone came to for the old system. That expertise doesn't transfer, and that's a real loss." This costs nothing and buys enormous goodwill.
- Mark the ending. A genuine last-day-of-the-old-way moment.
- Be clear what is *not* changing. People overestimate the scope of change; the reassurance is free.
- Don't defend the past. "The old process was terrible" insults everyone who ran it well for a decade.

### 2. The Neutral Zone — the messy middle
The old way is gone, the new way isn't yet fluent. **This is where productivity dips and where most programmes declare victory and leave.**

**Signs:** anxiety, drop in output, workarounds, spike in errors, conflict, people reverting under pressure.

**What helps:**
- **Tell people it's coming.** "Weeks 2–6 will be slower and more frustrating than now. That's normal, not failure." Predicted pain is tolerated far better than surprise pain.
- Short-term wins and temporary structures — daily stand-ups, floorwalkers, a visible help channel.
- Protect people from a second change landing simultaneously.
- Treat this as the creative window: the neutral zone is the only time people are genuinely open to new ways of working. It's an opportunity, not just a cost.

### 3. New Beginnings — the new normal
Not the go-live date. This is when people identify with the new way.

**Signs:** the new language used without prompting; people teaching each other; the old way referred to in the past tense.

**What helps:** the 4 Ps — **Purpose** (why), **Picture** (what it looks and feels like), **Plan** (step by step), **Part to play** (what *I* do). Reinforce early wins hard. Celebrate the first person who does it well without being asked.

## The productivity dip — draw this for your sponsor

```
Performance
   │  ────╮
   │       ╲                          ╭──────  ← new, higher normal
   │        ╲                       ╱
   │         ╲                    ╱
   │          ╲__________________╱
   │              ↑ the dip ↑
   └────────────────────────────────────────► Time
      Ending    Neutral Zone     New Beginning
              (where programmes
               get cancelled)
```

**Use this in the sponsor conversation before go-live, not after.** If a leader has not agreed in advance that a dip is expected and normal, they will interpret it as failure and start intervening in ways that make it worse — usually by adding pressure, which extends the neutral zone.

Ask them directly: *"Performance will dip for roughly six weeks. Can you hold your nerve, and can you hold the line with your peers when they panic?"* Get the answer in the room.

## Where this matters most in your three changes

| Change type | The unacknowledged loss |
|---|---|
| Tech rollout | Mastery. Your experts become novices overnight — and they're usually your most vocal people. Give them a role in the new world *first*. |
| New ways of working | Autonomy and identity. "This is how I've always run my team." |
| Culture shift | Belonging. People who thrived in the old culture are being told, implicitly, that they were wrong. |
