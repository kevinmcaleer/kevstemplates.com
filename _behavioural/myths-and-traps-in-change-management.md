Read this before your first exec conversation. Some of it will contradict things you've been told in training.

## Myth 1 — "70% of change programmes fail"

**Don't say this.** It's the most-quoted statistic in the field and it has no reliable empirical basis. It traces back to 1990s business-process-reengineering consulting material (Hammer & Champy, later echoed by Beer & Nohria) and was never supported by a peer-reviewed study. Mark Hughes' 2011 review is the standard academic takedown.

Three reasons to drop it:

1. **It's probably wrong.** Where it has been examined, most initiatives land in the middle — partial success — with genuine total failures a small minority.
2. **A sharp exec may know it's a myth.** Being caught citing folklore in your opening slide is an expensive way to start.
3. **It frames change as a lottery**, which quietly excuses poor design. "70% fail" is a comfortable thing for a leader to hear.

**Say instead:** *"Change initiatives rarely fail outright — they usually half-land. People get told, some get trained, and then the day job reasserts itself. Our job is to close the gap between the launch and the Tuesday."*

## Myth 2 — "People naturally resist change"

People resist **loss, ambiguity, and being done to**. They queue overnight for new phones. What's actually being resisted is usually a specific cost you haven't acknowledged, or a change that's genuinely worse for them. See [Resistance Decoder](/behavioural-change/resistance-decoder/).

Treating resistance as a personality flaw of the workforce is both wrong and strategically useless — it points to no action other than pressure.

## Myth 3 — "If we communicate well enough, they'll come on board"

Communication addresses Awareness and, at best, some Reflective Motivation. It does nothing for Capability, Physical Opportunity or Habit — which is where most rollouts actually fail. See [COM-B and the Behaviour Change Wheel](/behavioural-change/com-b-and-the-behaviour-change-wheel/).

**The tell:** when the response to low adoption is "we need to communicate the benefits better." That's the third or fourth email now. The problem is rarely comprehension.

## Myth 4 — "Training equals adoption"

Training completion is an attendance metric. The gap between "knows how" and "does it under pressure on a Thursday" is the entire problem — Knowledge vs Ability in [ADKAR — Individual Change](/behavioural-change/adkar-individual-change/).

## Myth 5 — "Get the burning platform right and people will move"

Fear creates urgency and a short burst of compliance. It also creates risk-aversion, information-hiding and blame, all of which are poison to the exploratory behaviour a new way of working needs. It doesn't sustain — you can't keep a platform burning for eighteen months.

## Myth 6 — "Change is a project with an end date"

The system goes live on a date. The behaviour transitions over months. The programme disbands at go-live; the drift starts at week six. See [Habit Loop and Behavioural Drift](/behavioural-change/habit-loop-and-behavioural-drift/).

## Myth 7 — "The culture is the problem"

Culture isn't a cause, it's an outcome — the accumulated residue of what gets rewarded, tolerated and modelled. "We need to change the culture" is usually a way of naming a problem without owning it. Push for behaviours: [Target Behaviour Definition](/behavioural-change/target-behaviour-definition/).

## Myth 8 — Learning-styles and personality-type framing

Matching delivery to "visual/auditory/kinaesthetic learners" has been repeatedly tested and doesn't improve outcomes. MBTI-style typing has weak reliability and validity. Both are common in change collateral and both will be challenged by anyone who's read the research. Don't build your credibility on them.

## The traps (things that are true but tempting)

**The Comms Trap.** Comms is visible, controllable and feels like progress. It becomes the default output of a change function. Ask of every plan: *what in here would change behaviour if nobody read the emails?*

**The Pilot Trap.** Pilot with a volunteer team, a great manager and unlimited support; it succeeds; scale it to teams with none of those things. The pilot proved the change works *under ideal conditions* — which was never in question. Pilot somewhere ordinary.

**The Steering Group Trap.** Governance expands to fill available time. Attendance at the steering group becomes the measure of engagement. Meanwhile nobody has spoken to anyone who does the actual work in six weeks.

**The Survey Trap.** A 40-question engagement survey tells you sentiment, not barriers. Six honest conversations tell you what's actually stopping people. Do the conversations first; use the survey to size what you've found.

**The Hero Trap.** One passionate person carries the change personally. It works while they're there and collapses when they move. If the change depends on you, you haven't finished.

**The Perfection Trap.** Waiting for the process to be fully designed before anyone sees it. You lose the chance to be wrong cheaply, and you lose co-design — which is the single best generator of Desire.

## What the evidence does consistently support

- **Active, visible sponsorship** is repeatedly identified as the largest single contributor to change outcomes — and "visible" is doing the work in that sentence. See [Sponsor Briefing Pack](/behavioural-change/sponsor-briefing-pack/).
- **Line manager involvement** — the employee's own manager is the preferred and most effective messenger for personal impact.
- **Involvement in design** raises commitment more reliably than any communication.
- **Making the desired behaviour the easy default** outperforms persuasion, consistently and cheaply.
- **Clear, specific behavioural asks** beat values and principles.
