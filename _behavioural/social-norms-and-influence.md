The most under-used lever in corporate change, and close to free. People take their behavioural cues from people like them, far more than from official communication.

---

## Two kinds of norm

| Type | What it says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | What people *actually do* | "Most teams in your region now log calls same-day" |
| **Injunctive** | What people *approve of* | "Your colleagues think same-day logging is the professional standard" |
{:class="table table-single table-narrow"}

Descriptive norms are generally more powerful for routine behaviours. Injunctive norms matter more where the behaviour is visible and social judgement bites.

---

## ⚠️ The single most common mistake

**Never publicise low compliance.**

> "Only 28% of managers have completed the new process."

You have just told 72% of your audience that ignoring it is normal — and given the compliant minority a reason to wonder why they bothered. This backfires reliably, and it appears on adoption dashboards constantly.

**Instead:**

| If the number is bad | Do this |
|---|---|
| Low overall adoption | Report a **trend**: "up from 11% to 28% in a fortnight" |
| Low in most places | Report the **best unit**: "Northern region hit 84% — here's how" |
| Low everywhere, no trend | Don't report a number. Report a **story**. |
{:class="table table-single table-narrow"}

And when a number is genuinely good, say it loudly and specifically — with the smallest credible unit. "Your team" beats "your division" beats "the company".

## Practical norm tactics

- **Make the behaviour visible.** Norms need visibility. If work happens in private, nobody can see the shift. Shared dashboards, visible queues, "who's done this" lists (positive framing only).
- **Local comparison beats global.** "Your team vs the site average" outperforms "you vs the company."
- **Name real people.** "Sam in Operations has done this for three weeks — ask her what changed" is worth ten leadership emails.
- **Use the credible messenger, not the senior one.** Influence is source-dependent. For a frontline behaviour, a respected frontline peer beats a director every time. See [Diffusion of Innovations](/behavioural-change/diffusion-of-innovations/).
- **Public commitment.** Specific, written, made to named colleagues, with a date. Dramatically outperforms private intention.
- **Consistency.** People act in line with their prior statements. Get them to articulate why the change makes sense *in their own words* — a two-minute conversation is worth more than an hour of being told.

## The leadership shadow

Everything a leader does is a norm-setting act, whether they intend it or not. The organisation watches behaviour, not statements, and it reads carefully.

Things that quietly destroy a change:
- The director who asks for the report in the old format "just this once"
- The exec who doesn't use the new system themselves
- The manager who says "I know it's a pain, just do it for now"
- The leadership meeting where the change isn't mentioned for a month

**One visible bypass by a senior figure is worth roughly six weeks of comms — in the wrong direction.** People aren't cynical about change; they're accurately reading the signals. If leaders don't do it, it isn't really required.

## The peer network layer

Between "leadership says" and "I do" sits the layer that actually decides: what my team does, what my manager tolerates, what the person I respect thinks.

Most change programmes invest in the first layer and the last, and ignore the middle. Build it deliberately — [Champion Network Playbook](/behavioural-change/champion-network-playbook/).

---

## An ethical line worth holding

Social influence works, which is exactly why it needs care. The test: **would this still work if the person could see exactly what you were doing and why?**

Making good behaviour visible passes. Manufacturing a norm that doesn't exist, or engineering shame, does not — and in a well-networked organisation it will be discovered and cost you the trust you need for the next change. Nudge in the open.
